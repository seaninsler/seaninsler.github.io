Enterprise Marketplace & Recommendation Engine

How I converted a low-adoption collateral portal into a broker decision-support system, secured enterprise funding, navigated governance, and drove sustained internal adoption in a Fortune 500 environment.

RoleProduct Manager
EnvironmentFortune 500 · Regulated
Starting PointPortal w/ No Adoption
End StateDecision Support · High Adoption

Context

Marsh is a global commercial insurance brokerage. The business unit I supported operated in a highly regulated reverse-flow consulting model, created after legacy insurer “kickback” structures were made illegal. Insurers now paid Marsh for legitimate consulting services and expected demonstrable value in return.

A basic DotNetNuke portal existed where insurers could upload marketing materials for brokers. It functioned, but it wasn’t part of any broker workflow and it had effectively no adoption.

The real job: create measurable insurer value by influencing broker behavior inside a system where brokers had no natural incentive to change.

The Structural Problem

Brokers did not need a document repository. They needed faster, defensible decisions when advising clients. Meanwhile, Marsh possessed extensive historical placement data across complex policy dimensions — data that could be used to inform which insurer best fit a given client risk profile.

The opportunity was not to “improve the portal.” The opportunity was to convert static collateral into a data-informed decision-support system that reduced guesswork and made it easier for brokers to position insurer options in client conversations.

  • Brokers guessed which insurers to approach, one by one.
  • Insurers struggled to see measurable value from consulting contracts.
  • The existing portal was disconnected from broker workflow and incentive.
  • The organization built software via external vendors and waterfall governance.

The Inflection Point

Leadership had a conceptual vision: use historical placement data to guide broker-to-insurer matching. What didn’t exist was a product strategy, a delivery model, or funding commitment that could turn that concept into a platform.

My first major challenge was translating that vision into a fundable enterprise initiative.

Executive framing that unlocked commitment

  • Established insurer confidence that the tool would create tangible placement value.
  • Reassured internal governance that even partial broker traction would still help demonstrate short-term value to insurers.
  • Positioned the platform as an industry-first advantage over major competitors.
  • Anchored feasibility in an existing insurer-facing data tool that could be translated to a broker POV.

The result was commitment to a large-scale build in an organization that historically did not build product internally.

Execution Under Constraint

At the time, most internal software was delivered by third-party consultancies using waterfall-style requirements and rigorous project governance. We had to decide whether to follow that mold or introduce a more iterative delivery approach.

We partnered with Oliver Wyman’s emerging software practice as a hybrid internal-external bridge that the enterprise could understand, and gradually introduced agile patterns over time — without triggering governance rejection.

Structural product decisions

  • Redesigned the data hierarchy and classification model to support recommendation logic.
  • Translated insurer-facing analytics into broker-facing workflows and language.
  • Built a recommendation engine that reduced manual guessing and one-off outreach.
  • Integrated marketing content and contact pathways into the same decision surface.
  • Supported proof-of-value reporting back to insurers (without relying on vanity metrics).

This was not a UI redesign. It was a shift in how value flowed through the organization.

Adoption: The Real Test

Adoption didn't begin with the recommendation engine. It began with a deliberate sequencing decision: brokers would never voluntarily open a new tool to try an unfamiliar workflow. They had to be given a reason to show up first.

We acquired professional insurance news content, personalized it using HR role and line-of-business data, and delivered segmented daily digests. Brokers started visiting the platform for something immediately useful to their existing work — industry updates relevant to their book — not because anyone asked them to explore a new product.

Once that visit habit existed, the recommendation engine had an audience. TrueMatch was introduced through prominent placement and contextual prompts inside a surface brokers were already checking. The cross-sell moment was built into a workflow they'd already adopted, not asked for on its own terms.

Adoption moved when the product reduced cognitive effort. Brokers described a client, received structured insurer suggestions informed by real placement history, and used those outputs to guide RFQs and client conversations — tasks they'd previously handled through guesswork and one-off outreach.

Within two months of launch, the platform reached 86% adoption across the broker population — the largest active internal user base of any optional application in the company.

What This Taught Me

Enterprise product leadership is rarely about feature delivery. It’s about incentive alignment under constraints: regulatory history, governance skepticism, vendor delivery models, global stakeholder complexity, and adoption resistance.

The breakthrough was not simply building software. It was translating abstract consulting value into a decision-support system that brokers voluntarily adopted — making insurer value visible and defensible inside a system that otherwise would not change.

In large organizations, adoption is earned by reducing cognitive and political friction — not by launching new portals.
