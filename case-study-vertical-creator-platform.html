The Most Important Decision Was What to Cut

How I identified behavioral failure risk in a 75%-built creator platform, ran a structured alpha to surface it before the client did, and made a scope reduction call that saved the launch — and bought a startup two more years of runway.

RoleSenior Product Manager
Team4 CS/Ops/QA · Eng Team
Client35-Venue Operator
OutcomeDelivered · Company Stabilized

Context

Advertease was pivoting from a marketing services agency into a product-led SaaS company. The cornerstone of that pivot was a verticalized content monetization platform — a club-native alternative to OnlyFans designed for nightclub performers, DJs, staff, and venue operators within a unified ecosystem.

A 35-location venue operator was the anchor client. Their sign-off was the condition for continued funding. If we failed to ship something viable, the company's runway was gone.

When I joined, the platform was roughly 75% built. Features existed. Payments technically worked. But it did not behave coherently. Navigation was fragmented, content types were duplicative, and creator workflows were overloaded with branching choices at every step.

The real risk was not technical. A product can pass QA and still fail in the market. The risk was that creators would encounter too much friction to activate — and the marketplace would launch structurally hollow.

The Core Problem

Leadership had a clear vision for what the product should be. What nobody had validated was whether creators would actually use it — consistently enough to build a fan-side value proposition worth paying for.

The behavioral unknowns were significant:

  • Would creators complete onboarding and meaningfully build out profiles?
  • Would they publish enough content to create real fan value before launch?
  • Were the overlapping content types causing confusion, or just surface-level noise?
  • Would less-experienced creators drop out at the points where the system required judgment?

Shipping without answering those questions would have hit a delivery milestone while leaving the marketplace structurally weak — and the client would have discovered the flaw themselves.

Approach

1. Inserting an Alpha Before the Required Beta

The contract required a client beta before sign-off. I proposed running an internal alpha first — not as a feature validation exercise, but as a structured behavioral stress test.

The goal was to expose creator friction before the client's staff encountered it, and to use real test content to seed the environment the client would eventually evaluate. It also gave us a defensible record of how we'd identified and addressed risk proactively.

2. Deliberately Removing Guidance Over Time

I organized approximately 20 creators into cohorts, each led by one of four internal team members. We started with detailed facilitation scripts, required written feedback reports after each session, and held recurring debrief calls.

As the alpha progressed, we intentionally reduced the specificity of our instructions — measuring whether workflows were becoming genuinely intuitive or whether creators were still dependent on guidance to navigate the system. This distinction matters: a product that works with hand-holding is not the same as a product that works.

3. Making the Call to Collapse Content Types

The alpha surfaced a consistent pattern: too many content types created publishing confusion and fragmented fan discovery. Creators hesitated at format selection. Fans had no coherent feed to anchor to.

I made the decision to consolidate all media formats into a unified "Post" model — a single content surface capable of supporting video, image, text, and polls.

  • Eliminated the decision overhead creators faced when publishing
  • Unified fan discovery into a single, scrollable feed
  • Created a foundation for future search and recommendation logic
  • Reduced long-term technical surface area and maintenance burden

4. Optimizing for Activation, Not Feature Parity

Beyond content type consolidation, we stripped unnecessary branching from profile completion and publishing flows. The aim was zero ambiguity at each step — not maximum flexibility.

One finding that stood out: experienced and novice creators converged toward nearly identical behavior once friction was reduced. The bottleneck was never sophistication. It was system complexity getting in the way of both groups equally.

Outcome

The client formally accepted the delivered platform. The company stabilized and continued operating for two additional years — iterating on the product and pursuing new clients.

The anchor client ultimately chose not to launch publicly, disengaging after delivery. That was a distribution risk we had knowingly accepted: their sign-off was the condition for survival, not a guarantee of launch. The decision to ship to an uncertain client was made deliberately, with full understanding of the tradeoff.

What the delivery actually proved: that a behaviorally coherent creator marketplace could be shipped under extreme constraint — on a compressed timeline, with an inherited codebase, and with no guarantee the client would take it live. The product passed the test it needed to pass.

What This Taught Me

In vertical marketplaces, product risk hides in behavior — not infrastructure. Payments can work. Features can pass QA. But if the workflow requires too much cognitive effort at any point in the activation sequence, the marketplace doesn't fill.

Simplification is not a cosmetic decision. Collapsing the content model changed the structural logic of how creators published and how fans discovered content. It was an architectural choice dressed up as a UX improvement.

The most important move I made on this project was not building something — it was running a test designed to tell me what to remove before anyone else found out it was wrong. Under existential pressure, the highest-leverage PM decision is often diagnostic, not additive.
