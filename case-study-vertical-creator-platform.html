De-Risking a Vertical Creator Platform Under Existential Pressure

How I refocused and shipped a club-native content monetization platform for a 35-venue operator, using structured alpha testing and UX simplification to reduce marketplace risk.

RoleSenior Product Manager
Team4 CS/Ops/QA · Eng Team
Client35-Venue Operator
GoalShip or Fold

Context

Advertease was attempting to pivot from marketing services into a product-led SaaS company. The cornerstone of that pivot was a verticalized content monetization platform designed for nightclub performers and venue operators.

A 35-location venue operator was waiting for delivery. If we failed to ship something viable, the company’s runway was in serious jeopardy.

The platform was roughly 75% built when I joined. Features existed. Payments technically worked. But the system did not behave coherently. Navigation was fragmented, content types were duplicative, and creator workflows were overloaded with choices.

The risk was not technical failure. The risk was behavioral failure.

The Core Problem

Leadership knew what the product was meant to be: a club-native alternative to OnlyFans, adult-friendly but not exclusively pornographic, capable of supporting performers, DJs, staff, and venues within a unified ecosystem.

What we did not know was whether creators would actually use it in a sustained way.

  • Would creators complete onboarding and meaningfully build profiles?
  • Would they seed enough content to create fan value?
  • Would the complexity discourage less experienced creators?
  • Would overlapping content types fragment both publishing and discovery?

Shipping without answering those questions would have satisfied a delivery milestone while leaving the marketplace structurally weak.

Intervention

1. Reframing Beta as Risk Management

The client required a beta before sign-off. I proposed running an internal alpha first, not as a feature test, but as a structured behavioral stress test.

The goal was to expose creator friction before the client’s staff encountered it — and to use real test content to seed the eventual beta environment.

2. Structured Real-World Validation

I organized ~20 creators into structured testing cohorts led by four internal team members. We created detailed scripts, required written reports, and held recurring feedback sessions.

As testing progressed, we intentionally reduced instructional specificity to measure whether workflows were becoming intuitive rather than dependent on guidance.

3. Collapsing Product Complexity

The alpha surfaced a consistent pattern: too many content types created confusion and duplicated surfaces for both creators and fans.

I made the decision to collapse all media formats into a unified “Post” model capable of supporting video, image, text, and polls.

  • Reduced creator cognitive load
  • Unified fan discovery into a single feed
  • Enabled future search and recommendation logic
  • Reduced long-term technical surface area

4. Designing for Activation, Not Feature Parity

We streamlined profile completion and publishing flows, removing unnecessary branching and limiting choice overload.

Interestingly, experienced and novice creators converged toward similar behavior once friction was reduced. The constraint was not sophistication — it was system complexity.

Outcome

The client formally accepted the delivered platform.

The company stabilized and continued operating for an additional two years, iterating on the product and pursuing additional clients.

The client ultimately chose not to launch publicly and disengaged after delivery — a distribution risk we knowingly accepted in exchange for survival.

What This Project Taught Me

In vertical marketplaces, product risk often hides in behavior, not infrastructure. Payments can work. Features can exist. But if workflows require too much cognitive effort, activation collapses.

Simplification is not cosmetic. It is structural.

This project reinforced that under existential pressure, the most senior product decision is often deciding what to remove — and validating that decision before the client discovers the flaw.
